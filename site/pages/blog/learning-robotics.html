Robotics you can watch, from high school to a master's

20 November 2026Beitlab Robotics

Most of us remember the moment a machine first made sense. A gear turned another gear, a line of code moved something real, and the world got a little less mysterious. We built Balance Robot v1 to make that moment easier to reach, and to keep it going long after the first lesson.

The robots are arriving faster than the people

Robots are no longer a far-off idea. In 2025, factories around the world installed 603,307 industrial robots. That was the first year above 600,000, and more than double the roughly 254,000 installed in 2015, according to the International Federation of Robotics. Figure 1 is that decade. The climb is not a smooth line, but the direction is obvious.

Most of those machines went to one region. Asia took 76% of the 2025 installations, 457,315 units. Europe took 12%, and the Americas about 9% (Figure 2). China alone accounted for 354,226 of them.

The next wave is still a forecast, and it is worth keeping that word in view. Goldman Sachs now projects roughly 890,000 humanoid robots by 2030 and about 6.5 million by 2035, a hardware market it puts near $138 billion (Investing.com). Figure 3 draws that scenario. It is not an order book. An earlier version of the same forecast was much lower, at 1.4 million units in 2035.

The harder part is the people. In the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, 58% of employers expect robots and autonomous systems to transform their business by 2030. The same report does not pretend this is painless. Broader digital access is expected to create 19 million jobs and displace 9 million. AI is expected to create 11 million and displace 9 million. Robotics is the one they expect to remove more jobs than it creates, a net decline of about 5 million (Figure 4). The report does not split that 5 million into jobs gained and jobs lost. It also lists robotics among the top drivers for seven of the ten fastest-growing jobs, and says it is raising demand for programming and systems thinking.

So the jobs change shape. Someone has to design these machines, program them, keep them safe and fix them when they fail. In the United States alone, Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute estimate that manufacturing could need up to 3.8 million new workers between 2024 and 2033, and that about 1.9 million of those jobs could go unfilled without the right skills (Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute, 2024). Figure 5 splits that 3.8 million. Most of it replaces people who retire. The same study found a 75% rise in five years in demand for simulation skills.

That gap will not be closed by one course or one level of school. It needs people who start early, keep going, and can see how the pieces fit. That is the part we think a robot can help with.

Robotics is usually taught in pieces

A dynamic robot is often taught in pieces that never meet. The mechanism lives in one class. The circuit lives in another. The controller shows up on a whiteboard, balancing a model nobody can touch. Each piece makes sense on its own. The robot, the thing that made you curious in the first place, is somehow missing.

Balance Robot v1 is built the other way: one small wheeled biped, with the motion, the board and the control in view together. A class can stop at the depth it is ready for. The names of the parts do not change from one room to the next. That is what we mean by easy to understand and still open to change. Nothing has to be hidden to keep it simple.

High school: watch first, then name it

Picture a group gathered around the robot as it crouches. Two legs fold, the body gets shorter, and the wheels stay right under it. Someone asks why the wheels don't slide out. That question is the lesson. The answer is a four-bar linkage: the knee stays close to twice the hip angle, and the wheel stays within a millimetre of under the hip across the whole movement. Students can see the rule before anyone writes it on the board.

The next questions come naturally. What keeps it standing? What happens if a motor keeps spinning? Why is there a red stop button on top, and why do the motors stay off until a safety chain is armed? Those are good first lessons: a mechanism, an actuator, a sensor, and a reason to be careful. They need a real machine to point at, not a graduate course.

University: the same motion, now as a model

The crouch that was a picture now becomes a model. Because the wheel stays under the hip, leg length acts mostly like height, so the balance problem looks like the one in the textbook: a body, an angle, two wheels. The hip servos set the height. Two direct-drive motors with no gearbox keep the body upright. A sensor on each knee measures the leg, so the controller does not have to trust the command, and an IMU on the board measures the body.

Timing becomes something you can feel. The motor drive runs at about 10 kHz. The balance controller, an LQR on the board's real-time core, runs at 400 Hz. Linux on the same computer sends the goals. Students can see which loop is allowed to be late and which one is not, on a diagram that matches the robot they just watched.

Simulation sits right beside the hardware. The robot description for ROS 2 and the Isaac Lab training tasks come from the same CAD as the crouch. A class can break things on purpose in simulation first, which is cheaper, safer, and the kind of simulation skill industry says it needs more of.

Master's: change the question, keep the robot

At this level the model itself is open to argument. Mass, inertia and delay become numbers you measure, not numbers you assume. The head and the chest camera raise the centre of mass and slow the fall. That is a specific claim about a specific robot, and a project can test it.

Policies trained in Isaac Lab are meant to run beside the LQR, while the LQR stays easy to explain. A thesis can try a new height, new gains, a camera behaviour or a locomotion question that is still, at heart, a balance question, without starting over on a new machine. Balance Robot v1 is a product, and the mechanical files and schematics stay with Beitlab. What we want to open is the learning path.

Why one robot, all the way up

Skills grow best when they have somewhere to land. A student who first watched the crouch in high school can meet the same robot again at university as a model, and again in a master's project as an open question. Each time the robot is familiar, and each time it asks for more. That continuity is how curiosity turns into competence.

The world is going to need many more people who are comfortable with machines that move, people who can think in systems, program with care and keep a robot safe around humans. We would like to help a few more of them get started.
